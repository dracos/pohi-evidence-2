In the appeals/CCRC referrals, I was instructed on behalf of Tracy Felstead,
Janet Skinner and Seema Misra, until December 2020. I then felt constrained to
withdraw from representing them. I ceased acting for them in the face of threats
made against me of proceedings for contempt of court, made by the Court of
Appeal Criminal Division, over the five-month period December 2020-April 2021.
The basis for those threats was never clearly articulated other than having some
vague ostensible connection with my having passed a copy of the July 2013
“Clarke Advice” to the Metropolitan Police on 17 November 2020, the evening
before a hearing on directions in the Court of Appeal. I continue to hold the view
that those threats by the Court of Appeal, made against my junior and me, had
no foundation in fact nor connection with any identifiable (still less identified) legal
principle. (The material in the Clarke Advice was material to which my clients
were entitled, as of right as a matter of law — later accepted by the Post Office
(the disclosure was said to be given by the Post Office pursuant to the principles
identified by the Supreme Court in R (on the Application of Nunn) v_ Chief
Constable of Suffolk Police UKSC [2014] 37.)) The Court of Appeal almost fatally
damaged the appeals and potentially adversely affected the appellants, because

I very nearly resigned from practice at the bar in November 2020 in protest
against the Court of Appeal’s in my view unprincipled treatment of my junior. I
was persuaded not to. Had I then done so, any argument on “second category
abuse of process”, as a discrete ground of appeal, without doubt would have
come to an abrupt end because no one else was interested in pursuing it and the
consensus view of all other lawyers involved in the appeals was that my analysis
was wrong, as was my clients’ concern to pursue that ground of appeal. (Each
had received extensive and detailed separate advice on the advantages and

risks.)

For a number of years, from the summer of 2017, I was unwell. For that reason,
I largely withdrew from my ordinary practice which was mainly in business law.
As a result of time on my hands, I wrote some articles on issues of law that
engaged my interest. Some of this was informed by the way that the courts in
my experience tend to treat small businesses rather badly in comparison with
large institutions. This perception in part was from my experience of swaps
litigation with banks. I was especially interested in the emergence of the odd
doctrine of ‘contractual estoppel’. I found the courts’ approach to these cases
resonated with Lee Castleton’s civil trial in High Court proceedings brought by the
Post Office in 2006 (a seemingly uncritical deference to a large, well-resourced,
public institution).

In the winter of 2019-2020, having read Fraser J’s judgments and the only other
reported civil decision, viz Lee Castleton’s case in 2006-7: Post Office v Castleton
[2007] EWHC 5 (QB), I became interested in Post Office miscarriages of justice.
That, in turn, caused me to become very interested in how the provisions under
s. 69(1) of PACE 1984, and the parallel civil evidence protections under the Civil
Evidence Act, had been repealed by parliament at the turn of the century on the
recommendation of the Law Commission. I found the timing to be of particular
interest, because it coincided exactly with the Post Office’s introduction of Horizon

and its energetic prosecution of its postmasters.

Separately, as a result of introductions made by individuals who had read an
earlier article entitled “Denialism: The latest entrants — Lloyds Bank the Post

I was so appalled by what Tracy told me in the course of a telephone call, that I
told her I would do everything I could to help her. When she told me, I should say
very reluctantly indeed - for her only stated interest was in clearing her name, that
for her imprisonment at the age of 19, for her experience of prison and for her

I told Tracy Felstead that, although I was not a criminal practitioner, I knew a bit

Since I had rights of audience, I told her that if she wished me to, I would represent

to the railings of Parliament in protest.) It appeared to me that she had suffered
a monstrous injustice, but that once the position was explained to a competent

tribunal her conviction would be readily and speedily quashed.

I could not then have anticipated either the procedural quagmire or the friction
and drag in the Post Office’s approach to disclosure that I encountered in the
Criminal Division of the Court of Appeal (disclosure to my surprise was provided
in a non-searchable format, so every document required to be located and
individually and separately read). I did, however, start to identify that there was
quite a lot more to the Post Office’s prosecution of its postmasters than at first
glance appeared. It was that interest that, in late disclosure given by the Post
Office in October 2020, elicited the disclosure on 12 November 2020 of the July
2013 “Clarke Advice”. This was largely as a consequence of my detailed study of
Seema Misra’s trial and the extraordinary delay in her appeal coming on for
hearing — some aspects of which are still little known. (For example, the decision
to prosecute her for theft was taken by the Post Office despite her willingness to

plead guilty to false accounting. This was because it was considered by the Post

After October 2020, when the Post Office by its Respondent's Notice conceded

I should add that previously, in summer of 2020, Mr Turner had expressed some
doubt/anxiety about the wisdom of some criticisms I had made of the settlement
of the Bates litigation, about which I believe the journalist Nick Wallis had written.
At the time of writing this statement I haven’t reviewed this, but I believe it was
because I had publicly questioned that, while the convicted claimants had
received no compensation at all from the Post Office under the terms of the
December 2019 settlement, they had nevertheless surrendered under those
terms all their heads of claim against the Post Office - save for a possible
(contingent) claim for malicious prosecution (but only in the event of a successful
appeal against their conviction). It seemed to me that the convicted claimants
would have been much better served by not being parties to the litigation at all. I
still hold that view. They never stood to gain an order in their favour in the civil
litigation (because it would be said to be a collateral attack on jury verdicts/guilty
pleas in the criminal proceedings) but nevertheless had surrendered most of their
claims. I still find it difficult to see the sense in that.

I infer from the circumstances that the reason Mr Turner was not called to give
evidence at Tracy Felstead’s criminal trial was connected with the asserted

I may be permitted to observe that the delay in these matters being fully and
properly considered, responsibility for which lies with the Post Office and its
lawyers and Fujitsu, has denied to this Inquiry, by death, evidence from a
witness whose direct evidence in my view would likely have been of
considerable assistance and value.

It is to be hoped, and Mrs Turner hopes, that Mr Turner’s concerns and anxieties
about the injustice of Tracy Felstead’s prosecution and conviction, an anxiety that
burdened him for almost 20 years, right up until his death in November 2021, may
now be properly considered by this Inquiry.
