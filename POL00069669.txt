Telephone attendance

Client: Royal Mail Group PLC Sub Postmaster Litigation

Matter: Mr Lee Castleton Matter no: 348035.134
Attending:

Name: Stephen Dilley Location: N/A Date: 22 November 2006
Start time: Units:

Thad a telephone conversation with Mr Castleton. Initially it was on an open basis during
which time we ran through my unanswered letter to him dated 20 November 2006. Running
through each paragraph of that letter as follows:

1 (a) I said that I was surprised that he inserted in paragraph 3 of the draft amended
defence and counterclaim that he did not sign the cash account for week 51 given that at
Tab 4 of our disclosure bundle there is a signed copy of the cash account for that week. He
said that his solicitors had got this wrong and it was week 52 that he had not signed. He
had not received a form P242 from the Post Office.

1 (b) I said that he was ordered to serve his amended defence and counterclaim on 6
November, but he hasn’t done so. I asked when he anticipated serving it. He stated that
he had only just got the papers from his solicitors Rowe Cohen. He has written to the court
to ask for an adjournment. This is because he is unwell. He understands that Mr Justice
Edie is back at court on Monday. He doesn’t really understand the amended defence that
has been prepared by his solicitors.

1 (c) I stated that pursuant to paragraph 3 of the order of Master Turner dated 23 October,
he has permission to make consequential amendments to the defence and counterclaim, but
the reduction of the counterclaim from £250,000 to £11,250 was not a consequential
amendment and that as such he did not have permission to make it. I then went on to say
that we didn’t of course object in principle to him reducing the counterclaim, but he needed
to agree to pay the Post Office’s costs consequential upon such an amendment which
includes the costs of abandoning the counterclaim. He said okay.

1 (d) I said that in the draft amended counterclaim that he sent to us, he stated that his loss
of income as a sub postmaster was £11,250. He said he had not disclosed any evidence
relating to mitigation of loss, for example how much income he has received from any sub
postmasters and if he has not received any, why not and what steps he took to obtain any
income. He said okay, he noted my request for information in this regard.

1 (e) I said that in my recent telephone conversations with Mr Turner, he said that he had no
objection to us having an extension of time to serve the amended reply, because he had not
yet served the amended defence. He again said that this was okay and did not appear to
object to the extension.

2. I told him that there was a pre-trial review hearing in London at 10.30am on Monday 27
November. I asked him whether he was going to attend. He wasn’t sure. I said I thought it
would be a good idea for him to go and he said that he would probably go. I said that I was
sorry to hear that he was unwell, but at that hearing we would oppose any adjournment of

incur after then were, if we were successful, going to relate to the costs assessment and
enforcing any judgment. However, if the trial is adjourned, this is likely to substantially

I asked him when he was going to serve us with his witness statements evidence. I said that
we really could do with this this week and would he get it to me by Friday. He hoped so. He
hoped to serve his witness statement sooner rather than later, but would not commit to a
specific date. I explained that unless we got his witness statements by 10am on Tuesday 28
November, we were going to apply for an order at the hearing on 27 November that he be
debarred from serving any witness statements. He reiterated that he would try to get the
witness statements served on us sooner rather than later.

3. IL said that he is out of time to serve his expert accountancy report and that if he wanted
to serve it he would need to seek the court’s permission. I asked him whether he was going
to serve it. He said he did not know and needed to read it.

Thereafter referring him to my second letter dated 20 November. He did not have that to
hand. Explaining that I had sent him a draft trial bundle index and wanted to know whether
he had any comments on it and whether he agreed with it. He didn’t have any comments
either way because he didn’t have my letter to hand. I said that we felt that it included all
the relevant documents. I said that if he thought anything else should be included, he
should let me know.

Thereafter I asked him if we could have a without prejudice telephone conversation. He said
that he did not know what without prejudice meant. I explained that it meant that I wanted
to talk with him about settlement and that anything we said had to remain confidential
between us and we could not refer to it in open court until after the conclusion of the trial
and after any judgment. At that stage, when the court came to make an order about costs,
we could then refer the court to it. He said okay, he was happy to talk on a without
prejudice basis. I explained to him that we had come very close to settlement and
expressed my regret that he had not felt able to instruct his solicitors to sign the Tomlin
order reflecting the settlement that he had proposed. He said that he did not know what
proposals they had made on his behalf. I explained that the proposal was that he paid the
amount of the Post Office’s claim ie £25,858 in full, plus interest. I also said that under the
settlement proposal, he agreed to pay the Post Office’s costs on a standard basis up to the
time in January of our CPR Part 36 offer and afterwards, on an indemnity basis. He also had
offered to make an interim payment on account of £30,000. I explained that the Post Office
would, under the terms of a settlement, write a letter to him stating that they didn’t make
any allegations of dishonesty against him. I also stated that in turn, the quid pro quo was
that he would write to them and say that he withdrew all his allegations about Horizon.

I said that incidentally, it was not part of the Post Office’s case, and never had been, that he
had been dishonest. I explained that to win, all the Post Office had to show is that he had
received a certain amount of money and stock, that given the transactions that had been
performed at the branch, he failed to account for money. The Post Office did not need to
show what had happened to that money, merely that he had failed to account for it. He
understood this. I said for what it was worth on my part, my private view was that he had
not taken the money. He said that if the Post Office succeeded against him, he would need
to bring a criminal prosecution against his staff. I said that that was not necessarily the case
and that it was also not the Post Office’s case that either he or his staff had taken the
money. He asked me what I thought had happened to it. I said that I had my own view,
but that it didn’t necessarily follow, that if he could not account for the money, that either he
or his staff had taken it. I said that in fact what had happened to the money, was irrelevant
to the issue that would be tried in the Post Office’s claim. He thanked me for indicating that
I did not believe him to be dishonest. He said that he understood the Post Office’s claim
against him could not include any allegation of dishonesty.

I asked him whether he was willing to settle on the basis that had been put forward and
explained that between now and the end of trial, the additional costs that the Post Office was
going to incur, were likely to be very considerable, over £100,000. I stated that if the Post
Office was successful and I believed they would be, that they would look to Mr Castleton to
pay their costs, I stated that given that the claim was for £25,858 plus interest, the costs
would well exceed the amount of the claim and it therefore made sense for the parties to
settle. He said that he did not understand the offer his solicitors had put forward and I
